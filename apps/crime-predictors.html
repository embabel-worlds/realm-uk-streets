What predicts crime?

Six official registers, joined on the district code, over the same 263 English districts. Tick and untick predictors below and watch what each one does to the others — a variable's apparent strength usually belongs to the variables it travels with, and no single model can show you that. Leaving ethnic diversity on its own, then adding density and deprivation back, is the shortest route to the point.

The model

Tick a predictor to put it in the fit. Alone is what that variable claims on its own; in model is what survives beside the others you have enabled. They are always shown together, because the gap between them is the whole point.

Reading the numbers. Each runs from −1 to +1 and says how strongly a district's crime rate moves with that variable, in like-for-like units, so the six are directly comparable. 0 is no association at all; + means more of it goes with more crime, − means more of it goes with less. Roughly: under 0.15 is very weak, 0.3 moderate, above 0.5 strong. Concretely, 0.566 says a district one standard deviation more deprived than average sits about 0.57 standard deviations higher on crime — with everything else held still.

The data, and where each number comes from

Six registers that do not know each other exist, joined on one shared key — the ONS local authority district code. Nothing here is stored: every figure is fetched from its source when you open the page, and the joins are made at query time.

  • Crime — ONS recorded crime by Community Safety Partnership area, year ending March 2024. The outcome is totalRate, the publisher's own offences per 1,000 residents, so nothing here is a population-size artefact.
  • Deprivation — MHCLG Indices of Deprivation 2019, average score. Higher = more deprived.
  • Density — Census 2021 usual residents per square kilometre.
  • Age — Census 2021, the share of residents aged 20–24.
  • Pay — NOMIS ASHE median gross annual pay, measured where the job is.
  • Ethnic diversity — Census 2021, computed as 1 − Σ(share²) over all five top-level groups: the chance two residents drawn at random belong to different groups. It is a property of the whole composition and is never any single group's share.

The view that runs

One saved view does the work, and the engine computes every coefficient — the page does no arithmetic:

CrimeModelWithPredictors(deprivation, density, youngAdults, pay, diversity, population)

MATCH (u:UkDistricts {set:'england-and-wales'})-[:LISTS_CRIME]->(c:DistrictCrime)
WHERE c.districtCode =~ '^[EW]0.*'
MATCH (u)-[:HAS_PAY]->(i:DistrictIncome)            WHERE i.districtCode = c.districtCode
MATCH (u)-[:HAS_DENSITY_STAT]->(d:DistrictDensity)  WHERE d.districtCode = c.districtCode
MATCH (u)-[:HAS_AGE_STRUCTURE]->(y:DistrictYoungAdults) WHERE y.districtCode = c.districtCode
MATCH (u)-[:HAS_DEPRIVATION]->(dep:DistrictDeprivation) WHERE dep.districtCode = c.districtCode
MATCH (u)-[:HAS_ETHNIC_GROUP]->(g:DistrictEthnicGroup)  WHERE g.districtCode = c.districtCode
WITH c, i, d, y, dep,
     1.0 - sum((toFloat(g.percent)/100.0) * (toFloat(g.percent)/100.0)) AS diversity
WHERE /* every predictor non-null, enabled or not — see below */
WITH c, [x IN [
       CASE WHEN $deprivation THEN toFloat(dep.imdAverageScore) END,
       CASE WHEN $density     THEN toFloat(d.density) END,
       CASE WHEN $youngAdults THEN toFloat(y.youngAdultPct) END,
       CASE WHEN $pay         THEN toFloat(i.annualPay) END,
       CASE WHEN $diversity   THEN diversity END,
       CASE WHEN $population  THEN toFloat(c.population) END
     ] WHERE x IS NOT NULL] AS predictors
RETURN regress(predictors, toFloat(c.totalRate), c.district) AS model

Why the sample never changes

Every predictor is required non-null whether or not you have enabled it, so all 263 districts are in every fit. Without that the sample would move as you toggle — turning pay off would readmit the districts pay is missing for — and a coefficient that changed would leave you unable to tell whether the model changed or the data did. That is the exact confusion this page exists to remove, so it would be a poor thing to build into the instrument.

What the numbers are

Every figure on the page is a standardized coefficient. Each predictor is expressed in standard deviations rather than its own units — pounds, people per km², percentage points — so that pay and density and an index can be compared on one scale without one of them looking larger merely for being measured in bigger numbers. A coefficient of 0.566 means: a district one standard deviation above average on that predictor sits about 0.57 standard deviations above average on the crime rate, holding every other enabled predictor still. Zero means no association; the sign is the direction. They cannot exceed ±1 in a single-predictor fit, and in a multi-predictor one they are bounded in practice rather than by arithmetic.

R² is the share of the variation between districts that the enabled predictors account for together. 0.529 means about half of why districts differ is captured here, and about half is not — which is the honest state of any model built from open structural data.

What "alone" means

The alone figure is the same view with exactly one predictor enabled. A standardized beta from a single-predictor fit is that variable's correlation with the outcome, so both columns come from one view and one method — no second measure, nothing to drift.

What an empty panel means

If the model panel is empty, the fit could not be computed — most likely a source did not answer, in which case the page says so rather than showing you a zero. A blank residual list with a live model means the fit had no districts far enough from prediction to name, which is not a failure. Zero predictors enabled is refused by the page, because a model with nothing in it is not a model.

What the appliance did here that a conventional stack could not

Six publishers, six formats, one join key, no ETL and no warehouse. Nothing was copied, scheduled or kept in sync: the page asks a question and the appliance fetches from each source, joins them in the query, runs a least-squares fit inside the query, and returns coefficients. Re-enabling a predictor re-fits over live data in about a second. The conventional path to this page is a pipeline per source, a schema to hold them, a job to refresh them, and a notebook to model them — and the answer would be as old as the last run.

Reading it honestly

  • Association across areas, never causation, and never a claim about any person.
  • Recorded crime counts offences where they happen and residents where they live, which inflates city-centre, retail and airport districts — Westminster above all.
  • England only: adding deprivation restricts the fit to England, because Wales measures deprivation on a separate, non-comparable index.
  • R² is modest by design. The joinable open data holds economic and structural variables, not the policing, reporting and opportunity factors that drive much of the remainder.
